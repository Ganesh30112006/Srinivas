Recorder Wiring

ESP32 DevKit V1 (ESP32-WROOM-32, 30 pins), one breadboard, an SSD1306 display, a MicroSD adapter, LEDs and buttons. Wire steps 1 to 5 now; step 6 is the microphone, for when it arrives. Pins match firmware/board/board.h.

Hold the board chip-side up with the USB socket on the left. The row that starts VIN GND D13 is then at the top, and the row that starts 3V3 GND D15 is at the bottom, as in the drawings. The board is wide: if it covers every hole on one side of your breadboard, run female-to-male jumper wires from its pins instead, or set it across two breadboards.

New to breadboards? Read the first three sections, then wire steps 1 to 6 in order.

How a breadboard connects things

You never twist wires together. Two things are connected when their ends sit in holes that are joined inside the breadboard. The numbers in the drawing match the list below it.

  1. Rails. Each long row along the edge is one strip. The row by the red line is + (3.3 V here) and the row by the blue line is – (ground). On some long breadboards the line has a break in the middle, which means the rail is cut in two there. Bridge the break with a short wire.
  2. Columns. Each short column of 5 holes (rows a to e) is one connection.
  3. The middle gap. Rows f to j form a separate column. Nothing connects across the gap. That is why a module sits across the gap: each pin then gets its own column.
  4. A part's two legs go in two different columns. This is how every capacitor, resistor and LED is placed.
  5. Never put both legs in the same column. That shorts the part and it does nothing.
  6. A wire joins a column to something else. Here a black wire joins a column to the – rail, so the resistor leg in that column is now on ground too.

4Status LEDs

Drawn for three ordinary 2-leg LEDs. Each one goes GPIO → resistor → long leg; its short leg goes to ground. Red means recording, green means ready, and the third LED means syncing. Red and green together mean "no network".

If an LED has four legs, it is an RGB LED and replaces all three. The longest leg is shared. If it is common cathode, the longest leg goes to the – rail; if it is common anode, it goes to the + rail. The other three legs each go through a resistor: red to RX2, green to TX2 and blue to D4. The bring-up test lights each pin both ways, so it shows you which kind you have.

5Buttons

One button runs the whole device: a short press starts, pauses and resumes, and holding it for 1.5 s stops. A second button is optional and just stops. No resistors: the ESP32's internal pull-ups are used. The board's own BOOT button also works as the main button, so you can test with no buttons wired.

Before you plug in USB

  1. Only the SD adapter's VCC is on VIN. No rail touches VIN.
  2. The display's VCC and GND are the right way round for your module.
  3. The 100 µF capacitor's longer leg is on the + rail.
  4. No part has both legs in the same column.
  5. The microphone's VDD goes to the + rail (3.3 V), not VIN.
  6. Each LED's long leg faces its resistor.
  7. Plug in USB. Windows should show a new COM port named "Silicon Labs CP210x". If none appears, install the CP210x driver from Silicon Labs.
  8. Flash the bring-up test (firmware/bringup). It checks the display, each LED, the buttons and the SD card, and shows the results on the display.

Pins to leave free

Don't wire these: D12, D15 and D2 (they set the boot mode, and D2 also drives the board's blue LED); RX0 and TX0 (the USB serial link). They are printed in red in drawing 1.

D34, D35, VP and VN are input-only and have no pull-ups. They are unused and stay free for later. D5 is also a boot pin, but it is safe as the SD card's CS because the adapter holds it high.
